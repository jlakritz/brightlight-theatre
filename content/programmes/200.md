  text: >-
    ## Two hundred years is a very long time.


    Long enough for generations to be born, grow old and disappear. Long enough
    for buildings to change, for forests to grow, for technology to advance, for
    traditions to be replaced by new traditions, and for things that seemed
    permanent to become nothing more than stories and memories.


    But what does a place actually remember?


    That was the question that started **200**.


    When the opportunity arose to create a brand new piece of theatre to
    celebrate the bicentenary of St Peter’s Church, I knew I didn’t want to
    simply tell the story of the building. Two hundred years of history is
    fascinating, but a list of dates and events isn’t the most theatrical of
    stories. I was excited to explore what might happen if we thought about that
    history through the people who had lived alongside it and through the things
    that a place might somehow remember. Instead, we have used the history as a
    starting point, the spark of a flame, to explore the universal human themes
    they revealed.


    And so the story we will tell is a story about what is passed down. The
    things we inherit, the things we change, and the things that somehow remain.
    Stories about love and loss, progress and change, belonging and community.


    And 200 has very much been a story about our community in its making, too.
    Our first month of rehearsal was dedicated to exploring the history and
    finding new ways to retell it. The plot lines you'll see tonight grew from
    those first workshops. Over the next month, the script started to come
    together, as Jeremy and Alison Lake and I plotted out the stories and wrote
    the dialogue, with the cast and company continuing to shape the stories and
    themes along the way. It was important we allowed local voices and
    experiences to find their way into the piece, and to ask what it is about a
    place like this that allows it to remain a centre of a community even as
    almost everything around it changes. A big thank you to everyone who
    contributed their memories or birthday wishes. You helped shape this play
    more than you could know.


    Mary Bennett’s wonderful histories of St Peter’s and Frimley, as well as the
    biographies she wrote of those buried in the churchyard, were another
    important source of inspiration for several of the stories and plotlines.
    They provided the factual threads from which we could begin to weave
    something new, taking real people and events and allowing them to spark
    stories that could belong to any generation.


    The result is a play that isn’t really about the history of a building. It
    is a little bit, but mostly it’s about the people who pass through it, the
    marks they leave, and the stories that remain. 


    I hope that whether you have been part of St Peter’s for generations, have
    only recently discovered it, or have no particular connection with the
    church at all, there is something here that feels familiar. Because although
    200 begins with a very particular place the stories we pass on belong to all
    of us.


    Finally, a huge thank you to everyone who has brought 200 to life. To the
    cast for their generosity, imagination and commitment - and special mention
    to Charlotte, Ann and Neil who are making their Brightlight debuts; to the
    crew for everything they have done behind the scenes; and to everyone in
    Brightlight who has contributed ideas and helped the play become what it is
    - our most ambitious new work to date.


    I hope you enjoy the show.

synopsis: >-
  A brand new play two hundred years in the making. Written in collaboration
  with the local community, celebrating the bicentennial of St Peter’s Church,
  Frimley.


  **She’s turning two hundred today. Her bones are woodwormed timber. Her joints
  are rust-set bolts. And she remembers everything.**


  200 follows Rosemaria - the beating heart of a Surrey village - older than
  memory itself, as she’s visited on her birthday by the stories she’s kept safe
  for two centuries. A winder of music boxes who loses her purpose overnight. A
  wife who writes letters to a husband who might never read them. A brother and
  sister squabbling over party bunting while their own family history spills out
  of a cardboard box.


  Bold, funny, and unafraid to break your heart, 200 moves between fable and
  village hall, between wartime letters and wind-up toys, asking the same
  question in every era: what do we keep, what do we let go, and what stays with
  us no matter how much the world changes around it?

interviews:
  - person: The true stories of 200
    subject: The history that inspired the stories of the play.
    q_and_a:
      - q: In the play, we meet Winder Billie who brings music to all of the village.
          What was this storyline based on?
        a: >-
          One of the stories we found in **Daisy Hills’ Old Frimley** that
          really resonated with us was that of Billy Guest, who worked as the
          manual organ blower at St Peter’s. The old organ needed someone to
          pump the bellows by hand, and Billy did this from behind a large red
          curtain, learning to anticipate exactly when the organist would need
          more air. During particularly long Bible readings, he would apparently
          try to sneak in forty winks, but he had to be careful not to miss his
          cue! But once the church installed electric lights, they realised they
          could also install an electrically blown organ. This meant Billy’s job
          disappeared and he was given the rather different responsibility of
          looking after the cycle shed. 


          That wonderfully human story became the starting point for **Winder
          Billie** - our own slightly more magical version of the person who
          literally kept the music going.
      - q: What about Florence and Jack who were separated by the war?
        a: >-
          Florence and Jack’s story was inspired by the real history of the
          Hollings family. 


          In July 1914, Lieutenant Jack Hollings became engaged to Florence. By
          October, following the outbreak of the First World War, he had gone
          missing at Messines on the battlefields of Belgium. Trapped in
          bureaucratic uncertainty, and never told that an eyewitness had
          reported Jack dead, his mother, Nina, channelled her grief into
          action. 


          Alongside Countess Helena Gleichen, Nina became skilled in radiography
          and offered her services to the British War Office. When they were
          refused because they were women, they took their mobile X-ray unit to
          the Italian Front instead. There, under artillery fire and often
          working in ruined buildings, they took more than 17,000 X-rays,
          helping surgeons locate bullets and save the lives of wounded
          soldiers. Their bravery was recognised with medals for military valour
          in 1916, although the radiation exposure permanently damaged Nina’s
          health. 


          Jack’s body was not identified for almost eighty years. Both Nina and
          Jack are now buried in the churchyard at St Peter’s - creating a
          remarkable real-life connection between their story and the place that
          inspired **200**. 


          For the play, we made the decision to change Nina into Florence,
          partly to keep the story simple and focused. But we wanted to make
          sure Nina’s remarkable story was not forgotten, so we gave her name to
          our modern-day party planner instead.
      - q: The Fable of the Stretched Mile is acted out with puppets. Was that storyline
          also inspired by history?
        a: >-
          Absolutely. It is perhaps one of the strangest pieces of history that
          inspired **200**. 


          Before St Peter’s was built in 1826, the people of Frimley were part
          of the parish of Ash and had to make the long journey to the church
          there for services, festivals and burials. For some, that meant a
          six-mile round trip on foot - through mud, rain, heat and whatever
          else the Surrey weather could throw at them. But the most difficult
          journey was made when someone died. Families had to carry their loved
          ones all the way to Ash to be buried. It’s difficult to imagine now:
          the physical effort, the grief, and the sheer distance involved. 


          Eventually, the people of Frimley decided they had had enough. They
          built their own chapel of ease, St Mary’s, at their own expense, and
          after an agreement with the Bishop of Winchester and the Rector of
          Ash, were finally given their own consecrated burial ground. In 1826,
          this ancient, timber-framed structure was replaced on the same site by
          the larger, stone church that still stands today. 


          That historical struggle became the starting point for The Fable of
          the Stretched Mile. Rather than recreate the events literally, we
          turned the six-mile journey into a slightly surreal story about
          distance, determination and the lengths people will go to when
          something matters to them. The remarkable thing is that the fable may
          look like one of the more fantastical stories in **200**, but its
          starting point is completely real.
      - q: There's also the play the villagers perform, called The Perpetual Curate.
          What's the story behind that?
        a: >-
          This one starts with a rather darker piece of St Peter’s history. 


          In 1850, the village was shocked by the murder of its perpetual
          curate, Reverend George Edward Hollest, who was attacked by burglars
          in his own parsonage. Hollest attempted to defend his wife and was
          shot during the struggle. He survived for two days before dying from
          his injuries, reportedly forgiving his attackers before he died. 


          The investigation that followed was remarkable for its time. Inspector
          Biddlecombe of the Godalming Borough Police carefully documented the
          crime scene, including footprints, mud impressions and discarded
          tools, and eventually tracked the suspects to a beerhouse in
          Guildford. Two of the men were eventually convicted of murder and
          publicly executed in 1851. 


          **So how did we get from a Victorian murder investigation to the
          rather solemn little pageant you see in 200?** 


          Well, that transformation is very much the point. The villagers in our
          play have turned the murder into an annual act of remembrance. What
          was once a terrible and very real event has, over generations, become
          a story about faith, courage, forgiveness and the endurance of the
          community. That also gave us an opportunity to do something quite
          different theatrically. The Perpetual Curate is a play-within-a-play -
          a slightly strange, solemn village pageant in which the Bell, Lantern
          and Door become characters alongside the Curate and the Shadow. It has
          a very different language and rhythm from the rest of **200**, because
          we wanted it to feel like something that has been performed and passed
          down for generations. And there’s something rather lovely about that
          transformation. The real story is about a man being killed in a
          terrible act of violence. Our fictional village has chosen to remember
          him not through the violence, but through what came afterwards -
          grief, forgiveness, resilience and the decision to keep gathering. In
          other words, the villagers have done what **200** itself is trying to
          do. They have taken something from the past and changed it into a
          story that means something to the present.
      - q: Was anything else inspired by the history?
        a: >-
          So many things. And not just the history - we spoke to a number of
          local residents, and almost every conversation gave us something to
          think about or something to include.


          Some of those things are obvious if you know where to look - the light
          through the windows, the thirteen bells, and generations of family
          history tied to the church. Others are much more hidden.


          In fact, although you might not always recognise it, almost every
          decision in **200** was informed or inspired by someone’s story, a
          piece of history, a memory, or something we discovered about St
          Peter’s and the people connected to it.
